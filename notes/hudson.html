Hudson River Gauge Check

Python · 2022–2025 data · 4 min read

The question

Can I trust a river gauge, and can I predict it? Above Troy, the upper Hudson and the Mohawk join and flow past a third gauge at Green Island. Water is conserved, so the three gauges should agree. If they don't, one of them is wrong.

Why it's useful

USGS river gauges feed flood warnings, water supply planning and research. If a gauge drifts, everything built on it is off too, and nobody may notice for months. Forecasting flow a few hours ahead also helps with flood planning.

What I did

  • Pulled four years of 15-minute readings from three USGS gauges.
  • Flagged bad data: ice, stuck sensors, spikes, gaps and duplicates.
  • Checked that flow in equals flow out, allowing for the time water takes to travel.
  • Built models to forecast Green Island flow 6, 12 and 24 hours ahead, and compared them with just assuming nothing changes.

What I found

On a typical day the gauges agree within 5%. From December 2022 to November 2023 they were off by about 2,600 cubic feet per second, which points to a calibration error at one gauge.

The best forecast was 20% more accurate than assuming nothing changes, 12 hours ahead.

What surprised me

The basic sensor checks missed a year-long problem completely. Every reading looked normal on its own. It only showed up when I checked the three gauges against each other.

What I'd do next

Run the same checks at other river junctions, to see how often gauges drift without anyone noticing.
